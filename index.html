03Proof of ConceptExecutive Summary · 3 of 7
Evidence

Our model is not theoretical. It has been built successfully twice at the state level.

Michigan proves it at the field-organizing level. Colorado proves it at the capital-and-institution level, and has now run for more than two decades.

Proof point one — Project 83, Michigan

In 2017, Michigan Democratic Party Chair Lavora Barnes (The 80 cofounder) launched Project 83 — named for Michigan's 83 counties — on three tenets: refocus on local parties and local candidates, retrain local leadership and grassroots activists, rebuild the Democratic brand in communities the party had ceded. Not a cycle-specific field program: a standing network of regional Political Specialists working year-round.

Why The 2024 Michigan Loss is Important

Infrastructure that survives a bad cycle instead of evaporating with it is the entire argument for treating this as an investment rather than a campaign expense. Project 83 is proof the model works. It is also proof the model needs patient, multi-cycle capital to reach its potential — Michigan built it inside one state party's existing budget. The 80's job is to do deliberately, with dedicated capital and a national blueprint, what Michigan discovered by necessity.

Proof point two — the Colorado Blueprint

Colorado was, as recently as 2002, one of the most reliably Republican states in the country. Beginning in 2004, a small group of donors — Pat Stryker, Tim Gill, Jared Polis, and Rutt Bridges, organized with the help of retired university president Albert Yates — built a coordinated funding vehicle that became the Colorado Democracy Alliance. The strategy and the reversal it produced are chronicled in the 2010 book The Blueprint: How the Democrats Won Colorado, co-authored by a Democratic journalist and a former Republican state legislator.

What Colorado Actually Built

A funding collaborative

Members-only, directing pooled donor capital to more than 30 aligned nonprofits and independent-expenditure groups — instead of one donor writing one check to one campaign.

One disciplined message

A consistent statewide message carried across every funded organization, rather than each group improvising its own.

Locally-rooted candidates

Contenders with genuine ties to their communities, not outside recruits parachuted into targeted districts.

Big-tent discipline

A shared willingness to set aside internal policy disagreements and unify around winning — The 80's own "Rebuild the Big Tent" principle.

The two proofs together

Project 83 shows what disciplined, permanent field infrastructure looks like inside a single state party. Colorado shows what happens when that infrastructure is capitalized deliberately, across a full ecosystem of organizations, and sustained for decades. The 80 is built to combine both: field infrastructure funded the way Colorado's donor coalition funded its own.

04What We Are BuildingExecutive Summary · 4 of 7
The solution

One 501(c)(4) with three integrated functions

Permanent capacity, assembled from the contested states outward.

FUNCTION 01 Capital Investment & Operational Support

Funds and stands up the organizer and regional-lead field structure. Partners with, invests in, or acquires existing state infrastructure rather than duplicating it.

FUNCTION 02 Agency: Media & Technology

Shared research, message testing, and creative capacity, so every state build carries one brand and real-time data instead of reinventing a messaging shop from zero.

FUNCTION 03 Civic & Political

Recruits, trains, and supports the candidates the infrastructure exists to elect. Rebuilds local parties and incubates activist networks.

Why a 501(c)(4)

The entity structure follows directly from the problem being solved.

  1. Multi-year commitments. A (c)(4) can hold and deploy capital on a five-year horizon rather than being bound to election-cycle reporting and spending patterns — which is what permanent infrastructure requires and what campaign committees structurally cannot do.
  2. Partisan flexibility within a social-welfare mission. A (c)(4) can engage in political activity as part of — not the entirety of — its mission, allowing The 80 to fund party infrastructure, civic organizations, and issue advocacy under one roof rather than fragmenting the work across single-purpose vehicles.
  3. Donor structure suited to patient capital. Contributions are not tax-deductible as charitable gifts. That is the tradeoff for the flexibility above, and it is stated plainly in every donor conversation and in the closing disclosure of every 80 presentation.
  4. A holding structure for a portfolio, not a single program. The 80 is meant to partner with, invest in, and in some cases acquire existing organizations — state parties, civic orgs, policy shops — rather than build every function from scratch. A (c)(4) is structured for exactly that.

06The Operating ModelExecutive Summary · 6 of 7
How a state gets built

Rome Wasn't Built in a Day

Michigan did not staff all 83 counties on day one. Project 83 grew into full coverage over multiple cycles. The 80 replicates that discipline deliberately rather than rediscovering it under pressure.

What phasing does that a lump-sum build cannot

It gives donors a visible, fundable proof point before committing to full-state cost — and it mirrors exactly how Michigan's own model actually developed, which is the strongest evidence available that it works.

Foundational operating tenets — from day one, in every state

  • Use local talent wherever possible; create a mentor/coach role while a young organizer comes up to speed.
  • Have every organizer become a notary.
  • Monthly, in-person, statewide organizer meetings — moved around the state, held midweek, each including a novel skill class such as first aid or self-defense.
  • A weekly call where organizers share successes and challenges and get input from the group.
  • Encourage cooperative programming between regions.
  • Debrief volunteers and staff from the top levels down — it makes people feel valued and drives participation.
  • Humanize the organization. Our people should be the face of the movement.
  • Keep diversity a top priority at every level.

What this figure does not yet include

The table above is people and platform only. It does not include paid media, event production, candidate travel, polling and primary research, legal and compliance counsel, office space, or software and equipment.

The September 2026 planning estimate for a full state launch — people, platform and program — was $6M per state, rising toward $10M with amplification. That figure is being rebuilt from the bottom up state by state; it is not yet a costed model, and this document does not present it as one.

How the template scales

STEP 01Cost the first state

Build the model in one state against real county counts and real salaries, and prove the number by spending it.

STEP 02Repeat, don't reinvent

The second state is the same template with different inputs. The negotiation is about county count and competitiveness, not about what a state build is.

STEP 03Compare like with like

Because every state reports against the same lines, a donor evaluating two states is comparing the same categories of spend.

Why a template rather than a plan per state

Michigan's model was discovered by necessity, inside one state party's budget, and it lives in that party's institutional memory. A template is how that becomes transferable — the specialist-to-county ratio, the training curriculum, and the recruitment pipeline written down once and applied everywhere.
